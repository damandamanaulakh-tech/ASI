# How the Government Really Works: The Hidden Patterns Behind Bharat's Project Failures

Birth and making real change should come first. Not optics, not announcements, not freebies.

We keep wondering why big projects get announced with massive fanfare but deliver half-baked results years later. Why policies look good on paper but fail on the ground. Why citizens always end up paying — in money, in emotions, and sometimes in lives — while the system moves on to the next big announcement.

The truth is uncomfortable but clear.

Most governments in India have operated without any real, long-term policy framework coming from the leader themselves. The vision, the structure, the implementation plan — these rarely come from the PM or CM's own ideological foundation. Instead, they come from big consulting companies, corporate lobbies, and policy networks that operate with one primary goal: Return on Investment.

These firms don't step into economically weaker districts or understand the daily reality of an average Indian citizen. Their frameworks are designed in air-conditioned boardrooms in Delhi, Mumbai, or foreign capitals. Many of the people sitting in top positions in these consulting setups are foreigners or NRIs who have never lived the life of a farmer in Bihar, a daily wage worker in Uttar Pradesh, or a small shopkeeper in a tier-3 town. How can they design policies that actually serve these people?

Every big proposal is sold to us with the same story: "This is for national security." "This is for India's global standing." "This is for Viksit Bharat." The individual citizen — their job, their land, their dignity — becomes secondary. When people raise questions, the answer is always the same: "You have to bear it. This is the cost of progress." Financial cost. Emotional cost. Sometimes the cost of lives. And we are told to accept it quietly.

The system is designed this way because it works for the people who fund it.

Elections are fought and won on emotional, religious, and economic fault lines. Voters who are struggling to survive are easier to influence with freebies, slogans, and fear. The real funding for political operations doesn't come from the common citizen's ₹10 or ₹100 donation. It comes from billionaires and large corporate houses who write massive cheques — not out of charity, but as investment. And like any good investment, it expects returns: favourable policies, land at cheap rates, regulatory leniency, and protection when needed.

This is why billionaires and pure businessmen struggle when they enter politics with genuine intent. Their DNA is profit and loss, not public emotion, not social justice, not long-term nation-building that doesn't show immediate numbers. When the system demands they choose between business interests and citizen welfare, the choice is rarely in favour of the citizen.

That's why most policies feel like amendments and adjustments rather than bold, original frameworks. They are calibrated to match international benchmarks, global ratings, and the comfort of foreign investors — not the lived reality of 140 crore Indians.

We keep believing that pressing the button on the EVM changes the system. It doesn't. The patterns are visible to anyone who observes carefully: the same consultants, the same funding networks, the same narrative style, the same way dissent is handled. The intent is never decoded publicly because the system was never designed for the common citizen to decode it.

This is why governments continue this way. Freebies are announced in hundreds and thousands of crores — not because they solve poverty, but because they look massive on TV and create temporary gratitude. A few leaders have tried to break this pattern. Most of them either got absorbed, threatened, or realised that the system has ways to make you irrelevant if you don't play by its rules.

The uncomfortable reality is this: the government has enough money, enough power, and enough machinery to make every citizen educated, skilled, safe, and economically secure. But if that actually happened — if every citizen became truly empowered — the current political game would lose its thrill. The drama, the division, the dependence would reduce. And for many in the system, that would make politics "boring."

In books, politics is about ideology, public service, and nation-building. In reality, it is often about managing narratives, distributing resources strategically, and keeping enough people angry or hopeful so that the game continues. Protests, Twitter outrage, toolkits, and high voter turnout don't change this fundamental equation — because increased participation without clear intent just means more people voting for the same old patterns.

This is why no major political force — not the ruling party, not the opposition, not even most activists — ever calls for a complete, ethical, 100% boycott or a fundamental reset. They ask for things that stay within the system's comfort zone. Because the system knows how to absorb pressure as long as the basic structure remains untouched.

Some people, frustrated with this cycle, start believing that dictatorship or a "strong leader" model might be better. The appeal is understandable — at least one person would be directly accountable, and decisions could be faster. But history shows that removing democratic checks rarely solves the problem of vested interests. It usually just concentrates power in fewer hands.

The system is not broken by accident. It is working exactly as it was designed to work — for the people who designed and funded it.

The question is not whether this will change. The question is whether we, as citizens, are willing to develop the understanding, the patience, and the courage to absorb the risks that real change demands.

Because freebies and announcements are easy. Real transformation is not.
