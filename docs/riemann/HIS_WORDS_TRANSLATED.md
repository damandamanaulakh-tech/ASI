# HIS OWN WORDS — Riemann, translated in full, to study the man

**Why this file exists.** The workbook held his sentences in cells, cut
short; the owner asked for the full words, translated, to study him — his
life and his working style, not the theory. This is that: his own
language on the left, a faithful English rendering on the right, and under
each a short note on **what it shows about how he worked**.

**Source, stated plainly.** Everything below is from *Bernhard Riemann's
Gesammelte mathematische Werke und wissenschaftlicher Nachlass*, ed.
Dedekind & Weber, 1876 (the full text scanned at archive.org,
`bernardrgesamm00riemrich`). Two honesty notes: (1) the German is from a
Fraktur scan; I corrected obvious OCR slips against context and marked the
few that matter — nothing was invented. (2) The **letters** survive because
Dedekind quoted them inside his biographical *Lebenslauf*; they are
Riemann's own words passed through one editor's hand. The **fragments** and
the **papers** are Riemann's own published/Nachlass text directly. The
English translations are mine, kept literal — where a word carries weight I
leave his German in brackets so you can weigh it yourself.

## PART I — HIS MIND: the philosophical fragments

*These are the private notebooks — his theory of what a thought IS. Nowhere
else does he say so directly how he thought thinking works. This is the
deepest window into his working style, because it is his model of the
working of a mind.*

### 1 · The main work (his own statement of what he was really doing)

> **German.** „3. Meine Hauptarbeit betrifft eine neue Auffassung der
> bekannten Naturgesetze — Ausdruck derselben mittelst anderer
> Grundbegriffe — wodurch die Benutzung der experimentellen Data über die
> Wechselwirkung zwischen Wärme, Licht, Magnetismus und Electricität zur
> Erforschung ihres Zusammenhangs möglich wurde. Ich wurde dazu
> hauptsächlich durch das Studium der Werke Newton's, Euler's und —
> andrerseits — Herbart's geführt."

> **English.** "3. My main work concerns a new conception [Auffassung] of
> the known laws of nature — the expression of them by means of other
> fundamental concepts [Grundbegriffe] — whereby the use of the
> experimental data about the interaction between heat, light, magnetism
> and electricity, for the investigation of their connection
> [Zusammenhang], would become possible. I was led to this chiefly by the
> study of the works of Newton, Euler, and — on the other side —
> Herbart."

**How he worked:** he did not think of himself as a mathematician who also
dabbled in physics. He thought his *real* work was one thing — re-expressing
all of nature's laws in new ground-concepts so their **connection** could be
computed from experiment. Mathematics, physics, and philosophy were three
faces of that single job. The word he keeps is *Zusammenhang* — connection.
Hold that word; it is the nerve of everything he did.

### 2 · Where he placed himself (Herbart, and where he broke from him)

> **German.** „Der Verfasser ist Herbartianer in Psychologie und
> Erkenntnisstheorie (Methodologie und Eidolologie), Herbart's
> Naturphilosophie und den darauf bezüglichen metaphysischen Disciplinen
> (Ontologie und Synechologie) kann er meistens nicht sich anschliessen."

> **English.** "The author is a Herbartian in psychology and in the theory
> of knowledge (methodology and eidolology); Herbart's natural philosophy,
> and the metaphysical disciplines belonging to it (ontology and
> synechology), he for the most part cannot follow."

**How he worked:** exact about his own debts. He names precisely how far he
walks with his teacher and the precise point where he turns off. He does not
absorb a master whole or reject him whole — he takes the method and leaves
the metaphysics, and *says so on the record.* (This is container 68 in the
brain-file — authorship-honesty — in his own hand.)

### 3 · What a thought is — the Geistesmassen ("mind-masses")

> **German.** „Mit jedem einfachen Denkact tritt etwas Bleibendes,
> Substantielles in unsere Seele ein. Dieses Substantielle erscheint uns
> zwar als eine Einheit, scheint aber (insofern es der Ausdruck eines
> räumlich und zeitlich ausgedehnten ist) eine innere Mannigfaltigkeit zu
> enthalten; ich nenne es daher „Geistesmasse". — Alles Denken ist
> hiernach Bildung neuer Geistesmassen. [OCR read *Basen*; context is
> *Geistesmassen*.]
> Die in die Seele eintretenden Geistesmassen erscheinen uns als
> Vorstellungen; ihr verschiedener innerer Zustand bedingt die
> verschiedene Qualität derselben."

> **English.** "With every simple act of thought [Denkact] something
> lasting, substantial, enters into our soul. This substantial thing
> appears to us, indeed, as a unity, but (insofar as it is the expression
> of something extended in space and time) it seems to contain an inner
> manifold [Mannigfaltigkeit]; I therefore call it a 'mind-mass'
> [Geistesmasse]. — All thinking is accordingly the formation of new
> mind-masses.
> The mind-masses entering the soul appear to us as representations
> [Vorstellungen]; their differing inner state conditions their differing
> quality."

**How he worked:** notice the word **Mannigfaltigkeit — manifold.** It is
the exact word from his geometry lecture. He is using the *same concept* for
a thought that he used for space. To Riemann a thought and a space were the
same kind of object: a unity that hides an inner manifold. That is why he
could move between geometry, physics, and mind so freely — in his head they
had one shape.

### 4 · Nothing once formed is ever lost

> **German.** „Die einmal gebildeten Geistesmassen sind unvergänglich, ihre
> Verbindungen unauflöslich; nur die relative Stärke dieser Verbindungen
> ändert sich durch das Hinzukommen neuer Geistesmassen." [OCR dropped the
> word *Geistesmassen* after "gebildeten".]

> **English.** "The mind-masses once formed are imperishable
> [unvergänglich], their connections indissoluble; only the relative
> strength of these connections changes through the addition of new
> mind-masses."

**How he worked:** this is his memory-law, and it is the owner's engine
principle stated in 1860s German — *the brains are memory; nothing is
deleted; only the strength of connection changes.* For studying the man:
this is why he never threw work away. His drawer of unpublished results was
not untidiness; it was this belief lived out — a formed thing does not
perish, it waits, its connections only re-weighting.

### 5 · Thinking has a location and a cost

> **German.** „(Nicht das Behalten unserer Erfahrung, nur das Denken
> strengt an, und der Kraftaufwand ist, soweit wir dies schätzen können,
> der geistigen Thätigkeit proportional.)"

> **English.** "(Not the retaining of our experience, only the thinking
> exerts effort, and the expenditure of force is, so far as we can estimate
> it, proportional to the mental activity.)"

**How he worked:** he treats thought as physical work with a measurable
energy cost — *holding* memory is free, *forming* new connections costs
force. A man who felt his own thinking as labor with a price, and who
therefore spent it carefully.

### 6 · The Earth-soul (how far the connection reaches)

> **German.** „…so werden auch die in den verschiedenen Individuen eines
> Pflanzengeschlechts erzeugten Geistesmassen … zu einem Gesammteindruck
> sich verbinden. Wie die verschiedenen Sinneswahrnehmungen von demselben
> Gegenstande sich in unserer Seele zu einem Bilde desselben vereinigen, so
> werden sämmtliche Pflanzen eines Theils der Erdoberfläche der Erdseele
> ein bis ins Feinste ausgearbeitetes Bild von dem klimatischen und
> chemischen Zustande desselben geben."

> **English.** "…so too the mind-masses produced in the different
> individuals of a plant-species … will join into one total impression. As
> the different sense-perceptions of the same object unite in our soul into
> one image of it, so all the plants of a portion of the earth's surface
> will give to the Earth-soul [Erdseele] an image, worked out to the finest
> detail, of its climatic and chemical condition."

**How he worked:** this is the speculative wing he *never published* — the
whole planet as one soul integrating the experience of every living thing,
the same way the eye's separate fibers integrate into one seen red surface.
For studying the man: he thought this seriously and kept it in the drawer.
His public papers are the tip; this is the ocean under them — and he had the
discipline to keep the ocean private until it could be made rigorous, which
it never could in his 39 years.

## PART II — HIS WORKING STYLE IN THE MATHEMATICS

### 7 · The famous halt (his proof, laid aside — in his own printed words)

> **German.** „Man findet nun in der That etwa so viel reelle Wurzeln
> innerhalb dieser Grenzen, und es ist sehr wahrscheinlich, dass alle
> Wurzeln reell sind. Hiervon wäre allerdings ein strenger Beweis zu
> wünschen; ich habe indess die Aufsuchung desselben nach einigen
> flüchtigen vergeblichen Versuchen vorläufig bei Seite gelassen, da er für
> den nächsten Zweck meiner Untersuchung entbehrlich schien."

> **English.** "One now finds indeed about this many real roots within
> these bounds, and it is very probable [sehr wahrscheinlich] that all the
> roots are real. A rigorous proof of this would certainly be desirable; I
> have, however, for the time being, after some fleeting vain attempts
> [nach einigen flüchtigen vergeblichen Versuchen], provisionally laid
> aside [vorläufig bei Seite gelassen] the search for it, since it appeared
> dispensable [entbehrlich] for the next purpose of my investigation."

**How he worked:** this one sentence is his whole method in miniature — and
it is the owner's halt-law printed in 1859. He does not hide the failure; he
*prices his confidence* ("very probable," not "certain"), *names the
attempts* ("fleeting, vain"), *dates the halt* ("for the time being"), and
*keeps walking* ("dispensable for the next purpose"). A failure is not a
full-stop; it is a halt with the loop left open. This is the Riemann
Hypothesis — born as a halt, not a claim.

### 8 · The geometry lecture — how he opens, how he closes

> **German (opening).** „Bekanntlich setzt die Geometrie sowohl den Begriff
> des Raumes, als die ersten Grundbegriffe für die Constructionen im Raume
> als etwas Gegebenes voraus."

> **English (opening).** "As is well known, geometry presupposes both the
> concept of space, and the first fundamental concepts for constructions in
> space, as something *given*."

> **German (close).** „…so führt uns dies in das Gebiet einer andern
> Wissenschaft, in das Gebiet der Physik, welches wohl die Natur der
> heutigen Veranlassung nicht zu betreten erlaubt."

> **English (close).** "…this leads us into the domain of another science,
> into the domain of physics, which the nature of today's occasion does not
> permit us to enter."

**How he worked:** he begins by pointing at the thing everyone treats as
*given* and refusing to take it as given — his signature first move. And he
ends by handing the question to the next science, naming exactly where his
own reach stops. He opens by widening the question and closes by marking his
own boundary honestly. Both moves, one man.

## PART III — THE MAN: the letters (his own words, via Dedekind)

### 9 · The habilitation ordeal, to his father (1854)

> **German.** „…Da entschloss er sich plötzlich auf mein wiederholtes
> Bitten, „um die Sache vom Halse los zu werden" am Freitag nach Pfingsten
> Mittag das Colloquium auf den andern Tag um halb elf anzusetzen und so
> war ich am Sonnabend um eins glücklich damit fertig."

> **English.** "…Then, upon my repeated pleading, he [Gauss] suddenly
> resolved — 'to get the thing off his neck' — on the Friday noon after
> Whitsun, to set the colloquium for the next day at half past ten, and so
> by one o'clock on Saturday I was happily done with it."

**How he worked:** he waited, he pleaded, he endured a sick and reluctant
Gauss — and recorded the whole small ordeal to his father plainly, even the
old man's grumble ("to get the thing off his neck"). He does not dramatize
himself; he reports. Note also: the examiner was dying, the timing was
cruel, and Riemann simply absorbed it and finished.

### 10 · The confession of his working method (to his father)

> **German.** „…doch habe ich zugleich gesehen, wie gross der Unterschied
> ist, ob man schon längere Zeit vorher mit seinen Gedanken in's Reine
> gekommen ist, oder noch unmittelbar vorher daran gearbeitet hat."

> **English.** "…yet at the same time I have seen how great the difference
> is, whether one has already, a good while beforehand, come to clarity
> [in's Reine gekommen] with one's thoughts, or has still been working on
> them immediately before."

**How he worked — this is the key sentence of the whole file.** He is
telling his father, in 1854, exactly how his mind must operate: the thought
has to have *ripened* — come to clarity well in advance — or the delivery
fails ("less fluent," he says of the part he wrote the night before). He
cannot improvise from the front of his mind; he needs the connection
already settled. His whole publish-when-ripe discipline is right here, in a
letter home, described as a thing he has just *learned about himself.*

### 11 · Learning to read the room (to his father, 18 Nov 1854)

> **German.** „…meine anfängliche Befangenheit hat sich schon ziemlich
> gelegt und ich gewöhne mich daran, mehr an die Zuhörer, als an mich dabei
> zu denken, und in ihren Mienen zu lesen, ob ich vorwärts gehen oder die
> Sache noch weiter auseinander setzen muss."

> **English.** "…my initial shyness [Befangenheit] has already fairly
> subsided, and I am growing accustomed to thinking more of the listeners
> than of myself, and to reading in their faces whether I may go forward or
> must set the matter out further."

**How he worked:** his weakest instrument — speaking to people — being
consciously trained. The shy man teaching himself, note by note, to move his
attention off himself and onto the faces in front of him. (The brain-file's
"weak container looped to strength" — here is the loop, in his own words, as
he runs it.)

### 12 · Dedekind's testimony — why he was hard to follow

> **German.** „Seine glänzende Denkkraft und vorahnende Phantasie liess ihn
> meist … sehr grosse Schritte nehmen, denen man nicht so leicht folgen
> konnte, und wenn man ihn zu einer näheren Erörterung einiger
> Zwischenglieder seiner Schlüsse aufforderte, so konnte er stutzig werden
> …"

> **English.** "His brilliant power of thought and prophetic imagination
> [vorahnende Phantasie] mostly let him take very large steps, which one
> could not so easily follow, and when one asked him for a closer
> discussion of some intermediate links of his reasoning, he could become
> disconcerted [stutzig] …"

**How he worked** (this one is a friend's witness, not his own — weighted
as such): he saw the destination in one leap and found the intermediate
steps a chore to reconstruct for slower minds. His gift and his difficulty
were one thing — the same large stride that reached the manifold and the
zeta-landscape made him stumble when asked to fill in the rungs.

## PART IV — THE END

### 13 · The last day, and the death (Dedekind's account, from his wife)

> **German.** „…aber noch am Tage vor seinem Tode arbeitete er, unter einem
> Feigenbaum ruhend und von grosser Freude über den Anblick der herrlichen
> Landschaft erfüllt, an seinem letzten, leider unvollendet gebliebenen
> Werke. Sein Ende war ein sehr sanftes, ohne Kampf und Todesschauer; es
> schien als ob er mit Interesse dem Scheiden der Seele vom Körper folgte…
> Sie betete das Vater Unser mit ihm, er konnte nicht mehr sprechen; bei
> den Worten Vergieb uns unsere Schuld richtete er gläubig das Auge nach
> oben; sie fühlte seine Hand in der ihrigen kälter werden, und nach
> einigen Athemzügen hatte sein reines, edeles Herz zu schlagen aufgehört."

> **English.** "…but still on the day before his death he worked, resting
> under a fig tree and filled with great joy at the sight of the glorious
> landscape, on his last, unfortunately unfinished work. His end was a very
> gentle one, without struggle or death-terror; it seemed as though he
> followed with interest the parting of the soul from the body… She [his
> wife] prayed the Lord's Prayer with him; he could no longer speak; at the
> words 'Forgive us our trespasses' he raised his eyes believingly upward;
> she felt his hand grow colder in hers, and after a few breaths his pure,
> noble heart had ceased to beat."

**How he worked, to the very end:** working the day before death, under a
fig tree, glad at the landscape, on the unfinished ear-paper. The loop left
open even by death — exactly as he laid the RH proof aside "for the time
being," he laid down his last work mid-sentence, and others took it up. He
watched his own dying "with interest" — the observer to the last.

### 14 · His own word on his faith

> **German.** „…die tägliche Selbstprüfung vor dem Angesichte Gottes war,
> nach seinem eigenen Ausspruche, für ihn eine Hauptsache in der Religion."

> **English.** "…the daily self-examination before the face of God was, by
> his own statement [nach seinem eigenen Ausspruche], a chief thing in
> religion for him."

**How he worked:** a daily audit of himself, conducted before God — the same
instrument as his mathematics (name what is true, price what is doubtful,
face what failed), turned inward, every day. The scientist and the pietist
ran the same procedure on different objects.

### 15 · The stone

> **German.** „Hier ruhet in Gott / GEORG FRIEDRICH BERNHARD RIEMANN, Prof.
> zu Göttingen, geb. in Breselenz 17. Sept. 1826, gest. in Selasca 20. Juli
> 1866."

> **English.** "Here rests in God / Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann,
> Professor at Göttingen, born in Breselenz 17 Sept. 1826, died in Selasca
> 20 July 1866."

## What to carry away, for studying him

One man, one procedure, run on four objects — space, matter, mind, and
himself. Always the same three moves: **refuse the given** (open by
questioning what others presuppose), **name the connection** (Zusammenhang
— the thing he was always really after), and **keep the loop open** (price
the doubt, date the halt, never fake completion, never destroy the
unfinished). He worked slowly, needed the thought ripe before he could
speak it, kept everything, published little, and died mid-work at peace.
The reticence is not absence of ambition — the drawer held a plan to
re-write the laws of nature. He simply would not say it out loud until it
was ready, and it never was.
