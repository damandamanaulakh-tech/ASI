# Leaf 22 — Riemann's own French fair copy of the 1859 paper

**Cod. Ms. B. Riemann 3, leaf 22r–22v.** Canvases 42–43, image ids `00000043`–`00000044`.
Gabcke's reference [12]: *"Sur le nombre des nombres premiers inférieurs à une limite
donnée, Fair copy in French by Bernhard Riemann (fragment), October 1859 or later."*

In Latin script, not Kurrent, and in a careful fair-copy hand — so unlike the draft
folios this reads cleanly. Confidence **[A]** throughout except where marked.

---

## What it is

The header, in his own hand:

> **Sur le nombre des nombres premiers inférieurs à une limite donnée.**
>
> Extrait des Comptes rendus mensuels de l'Académie des Sciences de Berlin.
>
> Communication de M. Riemann, correspondant de l'Académie.

Then straight into the mathematics:

> „Dans cette recherche, j'ai pris pour point de départ la remarque faite par Euler
> que le produit Π 1/(1 − 1/pˢ) est égal à la somme Σ 1/nˢ, lorsqu'on prend pour p
> tous les nombres ~~entiers~~ ~~Je désigne~~ premiers et pour n tous les nombres
> entiers."

(The struck words are his — he first wrote *entiers*, caught it, and corrected to
*premiers*.)

## Finding 1 — the thank-you is gone

The German paper opens with a paragraph of ceremony:

> „Meinen Dank für die Auszeichnung, welche mir die Akademie durch die Aufnahme
> unter ihre Correspondenten hat zu Theil werden lassen, glaube ich am besten
> dadurch zu erkennen zu geben, dass ich von der hierdurch erhaltenen Erlaubniss
> baldigst Gebrauch mache…"

**That paragraph is absent from the French.** Riemann, translating himself, cut the
courtesy frame entirely and replaced it with a two-line bibliographic header.

This matters because that opening paragraph is the single most-quoted evidence that
the 1859 paper was a thank-offering for the Academy election. In his own French
version the offering language is gone and what remains is a scientific extract. It
also fits what the letter on leaf 23r says: the paper exists because **Kronecker
asked**, not because the election required a gift.

## Finding 2 — *Grössengebiet* becomes *lieu analytique*

German:

> „…von +∞ bis +∞ positiv um ein **Grössengebiet** erstreckt, welches den Werth 0,
> aber keinen andern Unstetigkeitswerth der Function unter dem Integralzeichen im
> Innern enthält…"

French:

> „…prise depuis +∞ jusqu'à +∞ positivement autour d'un **lieu analytique**, qui
> renferme la valeur zéro, mais ne contient dans son intérieur aucune autre valeur
> de x qui rende la fonction discontinue…"

And again, a page later:

> „…prendre l'intégrale positivement autour du **lieu analytique** considéré, la
> prendre négativement autour du lieu qui contient le reste de toutes les valeurs
> complexes possibles…"

Twice — so it is his settled rendering, not a slip. **"Domain of magnitude" becomes
"analytic place."** He drops the quantity-word and keeps the place-word. The object
is not a range of sizes; it is a location in which the function lives.

## Finding 3 — an error in his own translation

German, identical in the 1876 and 1892 editions (checked against both full texts):

> „…so wie auch, dass sie verschwindet, wenn s gleich einer negativen **geraden**
> Zahl ist."
> — *that it vanishes when s equals a negative **even** number.*

French:

> „…et aussi qu'elle s'évanouit, lorsque s est égal à un **nombre entier** négatif."
> — *that it vanishes when s equals a negative **integer**.*

**"entier", not "pair".** As written the French is false: ζ(−1) = −1/12. The trivial
zeros are the negative *even* integers only.

I read the surrounding sentences against the German line by line and they track
exactly — including the paragraph immediately after, *„Si la partie réelle de s est
négative…"* against *„Wenn der reelle Theil von s negativ ist…"*. So this is a
genuine slip in the fair copy, not a misreading on my part.

## Finding 4 — he had "the real part of s", and used it

The French uses **„la partie réelle de s"** twice — for the convergence half-plane,
and for the negative half-plane — exactly where the German uses **„der reelle Theil
von s"**.

So the phrase *real part of s* was fully available to him, in both languages, and he
used it whenever he meant it. **He did not use it for the hypothesis.** There he
switched to the variable α and wrote that the roots of ξ(α) are **real**.

That is now confirmed three independent times, from three different documents:
the printed paper, the draft letter of leaf 23v, and this French fair copy. The
modern sentence "Re(s) = ½" is a translation of his coordinate change, not a
translation of his sentence.

## Finding 5 — where it stops

The fragment breaks off after:

> „…car alors l'intégrale devient infiniment petite pour des valeurs de s à module
> infiniment grand."

which is roughly one and a half pages of the eight-page German paper. It covers
Euler's product, the definition of ζ, Π(s−1)ζ(s) as an integral, the contour
integral, the equation 2 sin πs · Π(s−1) ζ(s) = i∫, single-valuedness, the trivial
zeros, and the contour remark for negative real part.

**It stops immediately before the ξ function.** The French version never reaches the
functional equation, never reaches ξ, and therefore **never reaches the hypothesis**.

## Dating — **[READING]**

Gabcke dates it "October 1859 or later". The header calls it an *Extrait des Comptes
rendus mensuels* — an extract from the published Monatsberichte — so it postdates the
printing, which Gabcke puts in spring 1860.

Riemann was in **Paris from 26 March 1860 for a month**, received by Serret,
Bertrand, Hermite, Puiseux and Briot, and spent a day at Chatenay with Bouquet
(Dedekind, *Lebenslauf*). A French fair copy of his most recent paper, begun and
abandoned in that window, is most simply explained as prepared for that visit. Stated
as a reading, not a fact — nothing on the leaf dates it.

## What this adds to the ledger

On **"what the world read vs what he wrote"**: the world reads a paper that opens by
thanking an Academy. Riemann's own French version of that paper does not thank
anyone. He removed it.

On **the words**: *Grössengebiet* → *lieu analytique*. When he re-expressed his own
sentence he moved it further from quantity and closer to place.

On **the notation**: he had "real part of s" and used it freely — and still did not
use it for the hypothesis.

On **the man**: he mistranslated his own trivial zeros. The fair copy is careful,
the hand is unhurried, and the error is still there.
